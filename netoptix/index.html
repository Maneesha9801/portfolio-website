The Problem

Network Engineering often feels stuck in a terminal-first world. At Ribbon Communications, I commonly faced a critical invisibility problem: routing protocols when configured without traffic engineering blindly prefer the 'Shortest Path' metric, often flooding a single link while alternative, higher-cost paths sit idle. When such issues were raised, diagnosing this congestion was a very exhausting process. It required SSH-ing into dozens of disjointed nodes, parsing thousands of lines of monochrome CLI logs, and manually correlating asynchronous timestamps to understand what was going on in the network. Essentially, I was forced to mentally render a complex, dynamic topology inside my head because the tools offered no visual spatial awareness.

The Friction

  • ⚠High Cognitive Load: Engineers must memorize IP addresses and cable connections.
  • ⚠Slow Diagnosis: Spotting a single "dropped packet" line in a 5,000-line log takes minutes.
  • ⚠Lack of Context: Text logs show status, but they don't show flow.

The Design Challenge

How might we transform network debugging from a task of reading and memorizing into a task of seeing and reacting?

User Research

To understand the root of the inefficiency, I interviewed 5 Network Engineers (2 Senior, 3 Junior) at Ribbon Communications. My goal was to map their mental models during a "Severity 1" outage.

“

I have to open 6 different terminal windows just to trace the error, if i dont copy the logs at the right time to a notepad and keep track of the flow, I have to start over.

— Senior Network Architect

“

I'm terrified of typing the wrong command during an outage. I dont know how to go about the process, and know which logs to look at.

— Junior Support Engineer

Key Insights

  • 1Invisibility is the enemy: Engineers trust what they can see. CLI logs hide the "state" of the network behind text.
  • 2Fear of "Touching": Junior engineers hesitate to run diagnostic commands for fear of breaking something. A read-only visual layer builds confidence.

Visual Iterations

Moving from a "literal" map to a "logical" map was a key breakthrough.

Iteration 1: The "Physical" Map

Initially, I tried to map nodes to their physical rack locations. This failed because "where" a router is matters less than "what" it connects to.

Iteration 2: The "Logical" Hierarchy

I reorganized nodes by their routing role (Core vs. Edge). This instantly clarified the traffic flow.

What I Learned

  • ✓Design for Trust: Engineers won't use a tool if they don't trust the data. Showing the "raw" config context alongside the visual abstraction was crucial for adoption.
  • ✓The Power of "Glanceability": Moving from reading text to recognizing patterns (color, movement) fundamentally changed how the team operated during high-stress outages.
  • Next:In the future, I would explore adding "Predictive Analysis"—using the historical data to warn engineers before a link becomes congested.
